# How to Audit Your Brand's Visibility on LLMs: A Practical DIY Guide for Marketing Managers

## Executive Overview

Large language models like ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google's Gemini are reshaping how consumers discover brands, yet most marketing teams have no systematic way to measure their visibility within these platforms[5][5]. Unlike traditional search engine optimization where tracking appears straightforward through ranking positions and click-through data, LLM visibility operates on fundamentally different principles. An LLM brand visibility audit reveals how large language models perceive your brand, which information they've indexed about you, and—most critically—whether they mention your company when answering questions that align with your target customers' buying journey[3]. This guide walks marketing managers through conducting a manual, zero-cost LLM visibility audit that produces reliable directional insights without requiring expensive SaaS tools or technical expertise. By the end of this audit process, you'll understand whether your brand appears in AI-generated answers, how frequently it surfaces compared to competitors, and what content gaps might be preventing visibility. More importantly, you'll have actionable data to guide your team's next steps in generative engine optimization.

## Understanding LLM Brand Visibility Audits: Definition and Strategic Context

An LLM brand visibility audit is fundamentally a measurement of whether large language models mention your brand when users ask questions relevant to your industry, products, and services[4]. This differs substantially from traditional SEO audits, which focus primarily on how websites rank on search engine results pages for specific keywords. When a user searches Google for "best project management software," the search engine displays a list of ten links in ranked order. The measurement is straightforward—what position does your site occupy? Traditional audits optimize for that ranking.

Conversely, when someone asks ChatGPT or Perplexity the same question, the AI synthesizes information from multiple sources and generates a conversational response that typically mentions between two and five brands[5][5]. There is no ranked list. The AI either mentions your brand or it doesn't. If it does mention you, your position within the response matters, but the measurement is probabilistic rather than deterministic—the same question asked moments later might produce a different response[35]. This probabilistic nature means your audit must account for variability by testing the same questions multiple times and looking for patterns rather than definitive answers.

An LLM visibility audit measures three core dimensions: frequency (how often your brand is mentioned), prominence (where in the response it appears), and sentiment (whether the mention is positive, neutral, or negative)[16][38]. Beyond these basic metrics, a comprehensive audit also reveals which specific queries trigger your brand mentions, which competitors appear alongside you, and which sources the AI cites when discussing your business[4][16]. For brand marketers, this is critical because it shows where your brand is missing visibility at pivotal decision points in your customer's journey. A brand might rank well in organic search for informational queries but be completely absent from the transactional queries where purchase intent runs highest[4].

The strategic importance of this audit cannot be overstated. Multiple studies confirm that LLM-generated answers are becoming a primary discovery surface for consumers[2][5]. When a buyer asks an AI "which accounting software should a mid-sized firm use," the brands mentioned in that response gain enormous credibility through implicit recommendation. Brands absent from that response simply don't get consideration. Unlike paid advertising, where you control placement, or traditional SEO, where you can optimize specific pages for specific keywords, LLM visibility depends on how AI systems have indexed and understood your entire brand ecosystem across the internet[36].

## Why LLM Audits Differ Fundamentally from Google SEO Audits

Traditional SEO audits are built on a foundation of deterministic, repeatable measurements. A keyword either ranks at position one, two, three, or further down the page. An audit captures that position, tracks it over time, and measures progress through ranking changes. The methodology is consistent. The same keyword on the same search engine produces the same result (or very similar results) every time you check.

LLM audits operate in an entirely different environment[2]. First, LLMs are probabilistic rather than deterministic. The same prompt entered into ChatGPT multiple times can generate different responses. One response might mention your brand first. Another might omit you entirely. A third might mention you in the middle of a list. This variability is fundamental to how these systems work—they're designed to generate novel responses, not retrieve predetermined rankings[35].

Second, traditional SEO audits can be automated fairly easily because the output is structured and predictable—a ranked list of ten blue links. LLM audits require interpretation. An AI response is conversational prose. You must read the response, parse whether your brand is mentioned (sometimes implicitly rather than explicitly), determine the sentiment, and identify the sources cited[4]. There is no automated extraction method that doesn't introduce error.

Third, the signals that influence LLM visibility differ from traditional ranking factors[36]. Google heavily weighs backlinks, domain authority, and keyword optimization. LLMs care more about consensus across multiple trusted sources, factual specificity, original research, and how prominently your brand appears in authoritative publications[44]. A brand mentioned once in the New York Times might carry more weight than fifty mentions on small blogs. An AI system evaluates whether independent third-party sources corroborate claims about your brand[21].

Fourth, the content retrieval mechanisms differ[2]. Google indexes your website and ranks specific pages within its results. LLMs use retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to pull real-time information when generating responses, supplemented by knowledge from their training data. Your old website pages might rank well in Google while new content barely registers. In LLMs, freshness and placement on authoritative platforms matter enormously because current citations carry more weight than dated material[44].

Finally, traditional SEO audits measure traffic directly—visits, clicks, conversions. LLM audits measure visibility and recommendation frequency, which translate to traffic and revenue through different mechanisms[38]. When your brand appears in a ChatGPT response, the user doesn't click a link embedded in the answer (though some platforms now offer this). Instead, they might perform a branded search later, visit your site directly, or inquire about you with their sales team. The attribution is indirect but measurable over time through increases in branded search volume and direct traffic[1].

For marketing managers accustomed to traditional SEO, this requires a mental shift. You're not optimizing for keyword rankings. You're optimizing for mentions and citations. You're not driving immediate traffic through search engine links. You're building brand awareness and credibility within an AI-mediated discovery layer that influences subsequent customer behavior.

## Designing Your Representative Query Set: The Foundation of Accurate Auditing

The query set you test determines whether your audit will produce meaningful insights or merely look rigorous[19]. A poorly designed query set—one biased toward your brand name, for example—will show perfect visibility and tell you nothing useful. A query set drawn from search volume data without considering customer intent might test questions nobody is asking. Building a representative query set requires thinking like your customer and considering every stage of their journey.

Start by identifying the questions your target customers actually ask when researching solutions in your category. This is not the same as the keywords you're trying to rank for in Google, though there is overlap[19]. A customer researching accounting software might ask Google "accounting software features" but ask ChatGPT "what accounting software do mid-sized firms use" or "how do I choose accounting software." The phrasing changes because the user treats conversational AI differently than a search engine.

Your first layer of query development should come from voice of customer research. Review your sales team's discovery calls, customer support tickets, and community discussions in forums where your audience congregates[19]. Which questions keep coming up? Capture these verbatim or close to verbatim. For a project management software company, this might include queries like "what's the best project management tool for remote teams," "how does Asana compare to Monday.com," "what project management software should small agencies use," and "project management tools for construction companies." These queries reflect actual customer language and real decision-making scenarios.

Your second layer should incorporate search volume data. Use Google's People Also Ask (PAA) boxes, which surface related questions users are asking, and filter your keyword research data to focus on informational and commercial-investigation intent rather than high-volume navigation queries[19]. If your keyword tracker shows three thousand monthly searches for "CRM software" but only fifty for "CRM software for nonprofits," the latter is likely more valuable to test because it signals a specific buying context where your relevance should be evaluated.

Your third layer should involve explicit category, comparison, and problem-solving queries. Include discovery prompts like "what are the best [product category] for [use case]" and "what [product category] helps with [specific problem]."[4] Include comparison prompts like "how does [your brand] compare to [competitor]." Include solution prompts that focus on outcomes rather than product names: "how can I improve team productivity" or "what software helps manage client projects." This diversity of query types reveals where your brand has visibility across the customer journey—awareness stage, consideration stage, and decision stage.

The right number of queries depends on your resources and how granular you want your insights, but aim for a minimum of fifteen to twenty five queries to start[18]. Fewer than fifteen queries introduces too much noise—any single outlier query can skew your overall visibility percentage. More than fifty queries requires significant time investment for manual testing. Fifteen to twenty five strikes a balance between statistical meaningfulness and practicality for a first audit conducted by a marketing manager in spare time.

When constructing your query list, aim for roughly fifty percent branded queries (queries that include your company name or product name) and fifty percent non-branded queries (industry or category queries where your brand is not explicitly mentioned)[4]. Branded queries measure whether AI systems have indexed information about your company and whether they mention you when directly asked. Non-branded queries measure whether you appear in discussions of your category or competitors without explicit prompting. Both matter, but they measure different things.

Structure your query set by funnel stage and intent. Create a column that categorizes each query as awareness, consideration, or decision stage. Awareness queries might be "what is project management software" or "how do I manage team workflows." Consideration queries might be "best project management tools" or "Asana vs Monday comparison." Decision queries might be "how to implement Asana" or "Asana pricing and features." This categorization helps you identify whether your visibility is strong at all journey stages or concentrated at specific points[4].

Document your final query set in a spreadsheet before you begin testing. Include the exact query, the category, the intent, and whether it's branded or non-branded. This discipline matters because when you're testing across multiple platforms and running each query multiple times, you need precise consistency. Small variations in wording can produce significantly different AI responses, so standardizing your queries upfront prevents that variability from contaminating your data[4][18].

Consider localizing some of your queries if you operate in multiple geographies or serve different customer segments. "What's the best CRM for small businesses" might produce different results than "what's the best CRM for enterprises." If geography matters to your business, include location-based queries: "best accounting software in Australia" or "CRM software for UK nonprofits." These reveal whether you have geographic visibility gaps.

Finally, document which platforms you'll test on before you start testing. The major platforms to include in any first audit are ChatGPT (the most widely used), Perplexity (which explicitly cites sources, making tracking easier), and Google's AI Overviews or Gemini, depending on whether you're testing generative experiences within search or standalone AI assistants[4][16]. If your industry skews toward technical decision-makers, add Claude (Anthropic's offering) which is gaining traction with developers and analysts. Testing consistently across platforms is essential because AI systems have different source preferences and retrieval mechanisms[19].

## Running Queries Across Platforms and Standardizing Results

With your query set finalized, you're ready to begin testing. The key principle is consistency and documentation. You want to treat this like a research study where every decision is defensible and repeatable, even if it's a DIY effort.

Start with ChatGPT, the most widely used platform for brand searches[13]. Create a free or paid account if you don't already have one. For each query in your list, open a new conversation thread (rather than continuing in the same conversation, which introduces context that might bias responses). Type in the query exactly as you've documented it. Let the AI respond fully. Do not prompt for additional information unless your documented protocol specifies follow-up questions.

As the response generates, read it carefully while it's appearing. Screenshot the full response or copy and paste it into your spreadsheet alongside the query. Completeness matters here because you'll later need to reference exact wording to understand how your brand was described. Before moving to the next query, note the timestamp. You'll want to know when responses were generated in case you need to track temporal patterns (do you get different responses at different times of day, for instance).

After testing all your queries on ChatGPT, immediately run the same queries on Perplexity. Perplexity's advantage for auditing is that it explicitly shows you which sources it cited in each response, making it easier to identify which websites are influencing your brand's visibility[4][16]. This platform difference is important. You might appear frequently in ChatGPT responses but rarely in Perplexity, or vice versa, revealing that your citation sources align better with certain AI systems' preferences.

If you can access Google's AI Overviews (available within Google Search for some queries), test your query set there as well. Note that Google AI Overviews don't appear for every query—some return traditional search results instead. This is valuable information itself. If your category queries don't trigger AI overviews in Google Search, that's a finding that tells you something about how Google is categorizing your space.

For maximum data integrity, test each query at least twice on each platform, ideally with some time separation (a few days apart)[38]. This accounts for the probabilistic nature of LLMs. If you test "what's the best project management software" on a Monday and again on Wednesday, you might get different responses. By running each query twice and documenting both responses, you can calculate how often your brand appears across multiple attempts, which better reflects your actual visibility than a single test.

As you document responses, be explicit about the format and structure. Note whether your brand was mentioned in the opening paragraph, in the middle of the response, at the end, or not at all. Note the exact order if multiple brands were listed—was yours first, second, third, or further down. Specify whether your brand was mentioned by name or only implied through discussion of your product. These details matter because position and prominence correlate with how likely a user is to notice and act on the recommendation[38].

Standardizing across platforms means documenting slight differences in how each platform operates. ChatGPT generates conversational prose without explicit citations in the response itself (though it can cite sources if you ask). Perplexity puts citations inline. Google AI Overviews format information differently from conversational AI. Document these platform-specific formats in your notes so you're not confused later about whether a citation was present or the platform simply didn't show it.

Create a template comment block you'll paste into your spreadsheet for each response. This might look like: "[Platform]: [Date/Time]. Query: [Exact query tested]. Brand mentioned: [Yes/No]. Position: [Opening/Middle/End/Not mentioned]. Exact mention: [Word-for-word text mentioning your brand]. Sentiment: [Positive/Neutral/Negative]. Sources cited: [List any sources mentioned in the response]. Competitors mentioned: [List other brands mentioned]." This consistency in documentation prevents you from having to reconstruct your memory when you analyze the data weeks later.

Throughout your testing, resist the urge to prompt the AI multiple times with follow-ups trying to get your brand mentioned. This introduces bias. If your brand doesn't appear in the initial response, that's the data point that matters. You're measuring what the AI offers without guidance, not what it will say if you coach it. Testing should reflect real-world usage where a customer asks one question and uses the response to inform their decision.

## Recording and Documenting Results Systematically

As you gather response data, you need a recording system that captures everything you need for later analysis without creating excessive busywork that causes you to abandon the project. A well-designed tracking spreadsheet should include all the information you need to calculate metrics and identify patterns, but no unnecessary information.

Your spreadsheet should have the following columns at minimum: Test Date, AI Platform, Query, Query Category (Branded/Non-Branded), Journey Stage (Awareness/Consideration/Decision), Your Brand Mentioned (Yes/No), Position (if mentioned), Competitors Mentioned, Sources Cited, Sentiment (Positive/Neutral/Negative), and Notes. The Test Date column helps you identify whether you're seeing changes over time. The AI Platform column lets you separate results by platform later. Query Category and Journey Stage let you slice the data by segment to identify patterns in where you have visibility and where you lack it.

The Your Brand Mentioned column should be binary—yes or no. Don't overthink this. If a human reader would recognize your brand in the response, it's a yes. If your brand doesn't appear, even if the response discusses a product similar to yours, it's a no. This clarity matters for calculating your core metrics.

Position, when your brand is mentioned, should capture where in the response it appears. You can use labels like "Opening" (first or second brand mentioned), "Middle" (mentioned but not first), or "End" (mentioned late or last). If the response lists brands in a numbered list, capture the rank. This helps you later assess not just whether you appear but how prominently.

Competitors Mentioned should list all other brands that appear in the response. This is crucial for competitive benchmarking. When you analyze the data, you'll want to know which competitors consistently appear alongside you, which ones appear instead of you, and which have higher positional preference. Tracking this from the beginning saves work later.

Sources Cited should note which websites or domains the AI pulled from when generating the response. On Perplexity, this is explicit. On ChatGPT, you might need to ask the AI to cite sources or review the response for indirect citations. The reason to track this is that it reveals which content is feeding AI recommendations in your category. If a competitor's website consistently gets cited and yours doesn't, that's a content gap you need to address.

Sentiment requires judgment but is straightforward. If the response describes your brand in glowing terms ("excellent for," "industry-leading," "highly recommended"), it's positive. If the description is factual but neutral ("offers," "provides," "available for"), it's neutral. If the response includes criticisms or caveats ("can be expensive," "has a steep learning curve," "not suitable for"), it's negative. Tracking sentiment reveals whether visibility translates to positive positioning or whether your brand appears in contexts where it's discussed as a lower-tier alternative.

The Notes column is where you capture context that doesn't fit neatly into structured columns. If a response was unusual—very long, very short, contradictory, or clearly inaccurate—note it. If the AI seemed to confuse your brand with a competitor or mischaracterized your functionality, document that. These observations help you later understand whether visibility issues are due to missing information in the AI's training data or poor positioning signals across the web.

Once you've completed all your testing, transfer your raw response data into a separate sheet or document you'll keep for reference. Create a summary sheet where each row is a query and each column is a platform, showing your results at a glance. This summary sheet makes it easy to spot patterns. If you see your brand is missing from all ChatGPT responses for decision-stage queries but appears frequently in Perplexity awareness-stage responses, that's a clear pattern worth investigating.

## Calculating Key Metrics: Turning Raw Data into Actionable Numbers

After you've completed your testing and documented all responses, you're ready to calculate three core metrics: mention frequency, share of voice, and average position. These numbers transform raw observations into comparable data you can track over time and use to guide optimization efforts.

Mention frequency is the simplest metric to calculate. Count how many of your total test responses included your brand mention. If you tested twenty queries and your brand appeared in twelve responses, your mention frequency is sixty percent[16][38]. Calculate this separately for each platform (ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini) because platforms often have different patterns. Calculate it separately for branded queries versus non-branded queries because you'll likely have higher visibility in branded queries (as you should—people are asking about you explicitly). Calculate it separately by journey stage to reveal whether your visibility is balanced across the customer journey or concentrated at specific points.

Share of voice (SOV) is a comparative metric that measures your visibility relative to a primary competitor. If your brand appeared in twelve of twenty responses and your primary competitor appeared in fifteen of twenty responses, your share of voice relative to that competitor is approximately forty-four percent (twelve divided by twelve plus fifteen)[19][38]. This metric answers the question: "Of the AI mentions that go to our brand or these competitors, what percentage goes to us?" SOV provides perspective on competitive positioning. You might have low absolute visibility, but if all your competitors also have low visibility, your relative position might be acceptable. Conversely, you might appear in forty percent of queries but discover a competitor appears in sixty percent, meaning you're losing half your potential mentions to that competitor.

Calculate SOV for your top two to three competitors. The formula is straightforward: your mentions divided by (your mentions plus competitor mentions) times one hundred. Do this at the platform level and at the overall level. If you have sixty percent SOV in ChatGPT but only twenty-five percent in Perplexity, that's actionable intelligence suggesting your content or positioning resonates differently with these systems' retrieval mechanisms.

Average position is trickier because not all responses rank brands the same way. Some list recommendations in order. Some discuss brands in prose without explicit ranking. Some mention your brand in different parts of the response depending on how the AI structured its answer. Despite these challenges, tracking position matters because mention placement correlates with user attention[38]. A brand mentioned first in a recommendation list gets more attention than one mentioned fifth.

For responses that explicitly list brands in rank order, capture the position as a number. For responses that mention your brand in prose within the context of a longer discussion, assign position based on approximate ordering. If the AI discusses Brand A, Brand B, and your brand in that order, your position is three. If you're mentioned in the opening paragraph as the primary recommendation, position is one. Average these positions across all responses where your brand was mentioned to get your average position metric. This isn't perfect, but it provides a directional signal. If your average position is three and a competitor's is one-point-five, that competitor is getting more favorable placement.

Sentiment distribution shows you how your brand is being discussed qualitatively. Calculate the percentage of mentions that are positive, neutral, and negative. If eighty percent of your mentions are neutral, ten percent positive, and ten percent negative, that suggests your brand appears factually but not enthusiastically in AI responses. Compare this to competitors. If a competitor has sixty percent positive mentions and you have ten percent, you have a brand perception problem that content alone won't solve—you need earned media and third-party validation to shift how AI systems describe you.

Frequency by journey stage reveals your visibility gaps. Create a mini-analysis showing mention frequency for awareness-stage queries, consideration-stage queries, and decision-stage queries separately. Many brands discover they're invisible at the decision stage despite good visibility in awareness. This is a critical finding because awareness without decision-stage visibility doesn't drive sales.

Frequency by query type (branded versus non-branded) is equally revealing. If your mention frequency is eighty percent for branded queries but only twenty percent for non-branded category queries, you have a discovery problem. People asking directly about your brand know to look for you in AI answers, but people researching your category aren't finding you. This suggests your brand isn't being recognized as an authoritative player in your category space.

Finally, calculate citation source overlap. Track which domains consistently get cited when your brand is mentioned versus which domains get cited when you don't appear but competitors do. If TechCrunch articles about competitors consistently get cited but your press coverage never does, that's a pattern. It suggests your earned media placement isn't reaching the sources that feed AI systems, or you're not getting coverage on the right platforms.

Document all these calculations in a summary report you can reference and share with stakeholders. Use simple language to explain what each metric means. For instance: "Our mention frequency across all queries is forty-five percent. This means on average, when users ask AI systems about products in our category, we appear in less than half the responses. Our share of voice relative to Competitor A is thirty-five percent, meaning we're mentioned significantly less often than they are."

## Benchmarking Against Competitors: Understanding Relative Positioning

Benchmarking against two to three primary competitors provides context for your metrics. A forty-five percent mention frequency might sound low, but if all competitors also hover around forty percent, it's actually a healthy position. Alternatively, if competitors are consistently appearing in seventy percent of responses, your forty-five percent reveals a significant visibility gap.

Select your competitive set carefully. These should be direct competitors in the minds of your customers, not necessarily your internal definition of competitors. The best way to identify this is to ask your sales team: "Who do customers compare us to when they're evaluating solutions?" and "Who do we lose deals to most frequently?" Rank these three or four companies by frequency mentioned, then focus your benchmarking audit on the top competitor you want to beat.

Run the exact same twenty-five query set against your top three competitors. Test what ChatGPT says when you ask "what's the best CRM software" or "how does CRM A compare to CRM B." For each competitor, conduct the same two test runs on each platform and document the data identically to your own audit.

When benchmarking, look for three patterns: volume differences, position differences, and sentiment differences. Volume differences reveal whether you're simply being mentioned less often. Position differences reveal whether when you do appear, you're getting lower placement. Sentiment differences reveal whether you're described less favorably even when mentioned.

Create a competitive comparison table that shows each brand's mention frequency by platform, mention frequency by journey stage, and average position when mentioned. This table becomes your competitive intelligence dashboard. Share it with your leadership team alongside your own audit findings. "Against Competitor A, we have thirty-five percent share of voice in ChatGPT but only eighteen percent in Perplexity" becomes a conversation-starting finding that justifies investment in specific optimizations.

Use competitor benchmarking not just to assess your competitive position but also to identify what's working in your category. Which competitors appear most often? What content are they publishing? Where are they getting mentioned that you aren't? How do AI systems describe them? These aren't questions you'll answer in a first audit—they point to deeper research you might conduct afterward—but the benchmarking data creates the foundation for those investigations.

## Understanding LLMs.txt Files: The Reality Versus the Hype

One term you'll encounter in discussions of LLM optimization is "LLMs.txt" files. These have generated significant discussion in the SEO community, with strong opinions on both sides. For your audit and optimization strategy, it's worth understanding what they are, what the evidence suggests about their effectiveness, and whether they belong in your audit process.

An LLMs.txt file is a simple text file—similar to robots.txt—that you place in your website's root directory to guide AI systems toward your most important content[7][10]. The concept is that instead of making AI crawlers wade through your entire website structure, navigation menus, and JavaScript, you provide a summary in plain text or markdown format highlighting your most valuable content and core business information. The theory is that this helps AI systems extract the right information about you more efficiently and accurately, reducing hallucinations and improving your visibility in AI responses[7].

The proposal originated with the idea of creating an emerging standard similar to robots.txt, which search engines have respected for decades[8][10]. Several early adopters, including technology companies like Cloudflare, Vercel, and Anthropic, began publishing LLMs.txt files on their websites[10]. This generated a sense that this was becoming an industry standard worth adopting.

However, the evidence on LLMs.txt effectiveness is mixed and decidedly inconclusive[8][10]. The critical problem is that no major AI platform—not OpenAI, not Google, not Anthropic, not Perplexity—has officially stated that they use LLMs.txt files to inform content retrieval or ranking in their responses[10]. Testing by SEO researchers found barely any evidence that AI bots are actually fetching or using these files[8]. One analysis noted that despite the growing hype, most AI bots don't recognize LLMs.txt as a viable protocol[8].

Complicating matters further, Google explicitly stated it does not support LLMs.txt and gives it no special consideration in its algorithms[8]. Yet shortly after this statement, several Google developer pages appeared with LLMs.txt files published, then quickly removed them[8]. This mixed messaging from the industry leader itself has created confusion about whether the protocol has any actual value.

The argument in favor of LLMs.txt files remains somewhat sound from a theoretical perspective. Rendering JavaScript and extracting meaningful content from complex website structures is computationally expensive for AI systems operating at scale. Providing a clean, markdown-formatted summary of your core content and key information could theoretically help AI systems understand your business more clearly without consuming unnecessary processing resources[8][10]. Furthermore, if LLMs.txt does become a recognized standard in the future, having one in place now positions you ahead of the curve.

However, the practical reality is that LLMs.txt files are not yet a proven optimization tactic[8]. One SEO researcher studying the topic concluded: "It's too early to recommend that site owners add an LLMs.txt file as standard practice and feed into the dubious and ambiguous narratives driving the SEO space."[8] Most major AI platforms can crawl and understand your website structure without needing an LLMs.txt file. Creating and maintaining one adds work with unclear benefit.

For your DIY audit, you don't need to implement an LLMs.txt file to measure your current visibility. If you want to experiment with creating one, it won't hurt (as long as you maintain it accurately), but treat it as a low-priority experiment rather than a critical optimization tactic[7][10]. If you do decide to create one, include your company summary, your core products or services, links to your most important pages, and any proprietary data or research you want highlighted[7]. Place it at the root of your domain (example.com/llms.txt) and ensure the formatting is clean and readable. But don't invest significant resources in this until there's stronger evidence of platform adoption.

The more important implication of the LLMs.txt discussion is this: your basic website structure, content clarity, and content extractability matter. AI systems need to be able to understand what your business does, who you serve, and what you offer. Whether they do that through an LLMs.txt file, through crawling your website structure, or through analyzing your content across multiple pages is secondary. Focus your optimization efforts on ensuring your website clearly explains your value proposition, correctly structures information, and makes your expertise obvious—with or without an LLMs.txt file.

## Building Your Audit Tracking Template and Spreadsheet Structure

A well-designed spreadsheet becomes your primary asset throughout this audit process and beyond. It serves as your data collection tool, your calculation engine, and your reference document for understanding what you've learned.

At the most basic level, your spreadsheet needs to track the following columns: (1) Test Date, (2) Query, (3) Query Type (Branded/Non-Branded), (4) Journey Stage (Awareness/Consideration/Decision), (5) Platform (ChatGPT/Perplexity/Gemini), (6) Your Brand Mentioned (Yes/No), (7) Position (1st/2nd/3rd/Not mentioned), (8) Competitors Mentioned, (9) Sentiment (Positive/Neutral/Negative), (10) Sources Cited, (11) Full Response Text (pasted directly from the AI), and (12) Notes.

Create this structure as your "Raw Data" sheet. Every single test you run gets documented here. You want this sheet to be complete enough that someone else could look at it and understand exactly what test was conducted, what the result was, and what the AI said. This level of documentation might seem excessive, but it's essential for two reasons: first, weeks later you'll forget the context of individual responses and need to refer back; second, if you need to show your work to skeptical stakeholders, you have evidence to back up your claims.

Create a separate sheet called "Summary by Query" where each row is one of your original queries and columns show the mention frequency across platforms. For instance, the query "what's the best project management software" might show: ChatGPT 100% (appeared in both test runs), Perplexity 50% (appeared in one test, not the other), Gemini 100%. This summary lets you instantly see which queries drive visibility and which ones consistently exclude you.

Create a third sheet called "Summary by Platform" that shows your aggregate metrics by platform. This sheet displays your mention frequency on ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Gemini. It shows your average position on each platform. It shows your sentiment distribution. It shows your share of voice against each competitor. This is the sheet you'll show to stakeholders because it tells the story in numbers.

Create a fourth sheet called "Competitor Benchmarking" that shows the same metrics for each competitor you're testing. Use this to create a comparative table showing your visibility relative to competitors at a glance.

Add a sheet called "Findings & Recommendations" where you'll document your major discoveries and what they suggest you should do next. This becomes your actionable output from the audit.

Within your Raw Data sheet, use data validation and conditional formatting to minimize data entry errors. Create a dropdown list for Platform so you only enter "ChatGPT," "Perplexity," or "Gemini" (no misspellings). Create dropdowns for Query Type, Journey Stage, Position, and Sentiment. This enforces consistency and makes it easier to later sort and filter.

Use conditional formatting to color-code rows where your brand was mentioned (green) versus rows where you weren't mentioned (red). At a glance, this helps you spot patterns. If you see many red rows clustered together, they might be decision-stage queries or non-branded queries, revealing a pattern.

For the full response text column, you'll be pasting long text. Set that column to word-wrap so your spreadsheet doesn't become unwieldy. Consider using separate rows or a comment function to avoid drowning out the structure.

Create summary formulas that automatically calculate your mention frequency: =COUNTIF([Your Brand Mentioned column], "Yes") / COUNTA([Your Brand Mentioned column]) * 100. This saves you from manually calculating percentages and reduces the chance of arithmetic errors.

Create formulas that calculate average position. This is trickier because you need to average only the rows where your brand was mentioned and convert text positions ("Opening," "Middle," "End") to numbers. You might create a helper column that assigns 1 to "Opening," 2 to "Middle," 3 to "End," then average that column for rows where you were mentioned.

Keep your spreadsheet organized but not overly complex. The goal is to capture all necessary data without creating so much structure that data entry becomes tedious and you abandon the project. A spreadsheet that's eighty percent complete and actually finished beats a theoretically perfect spreadsheet you never complete because it's too cumbersome.

## Common Findings from First LLM Audits: What Brands Typically Discover

When brands conduct their first LLM visibility audit, several patterns emerge with striking regularity. Understanding these common findings helps you interpret your own results and know whether what you're seeing is typical or unusual.

The most common finding is lower-than-expected visibility in non-branded category queries[3][1]. Brands often discover they rank well in Google for category terms, yet barely appear when users ask AI systems about solutions in their category. For instance, a project management software company might have the top three Google rankings for "project management software" yet appear in only twenty percent of ChatGPT responses to that same query. This reveals a fundamental gap between traditional SEO strength and AI visibility strength[1]. It suggests that the signals that drive Google rankings—backlinks, keyword optimization, domain authority—don't fully translate to AI citations, which weight consensus across multiple sources and original research more heavily.

The second common finding is significant platform variation[19][35]. Your mention frequency on ChatGPT might be sixty percent while your mention frequency on Perplexity is only twenty-five percent, or vice versa. This happens because AI platforms use different retrieval mechanisms, weight sources differently, and include different training data[35]. ChatGPT pulls heavily from Common Crawl training data and doesn't do live web retrieval for many queries, while Perplexity prioritizes real-time retrieval from the live web. LinkedIn is the second-most cited domain on ChatGPT but less dominant on other platforms[13]. These platform differences mean you can't think of "AI visibility" as monolithic—you need platform-specific strategies.

The third common finding is visibility gaps by journey stage[4]. Many brands discover they have strong awareness-stage visibility—appearing in responses to "what is project management software"—but weak or nonexistent decision-stage visibility—appearing in responses to "best project management software for remote teams" or "how much does Asana cost." This is critical because decision-stage queries are where purchase intent runs highest and where visibility directly influences buying decisions. Audit results frequently reveal that brands are visible at the top of the funnel but invisible where it matters most for conversion.

Fourth, brands commonly discover they're invisible in comparison queries[21]. When users ask "how does Asana compare to Monday" or "Asana vs Slack," major platforms mention one software but not the other. If you're frequently omitted from comparison conversations about you and competitors, that's a sign that your content positioning isn't clear enough or your earned media presence isn't strong enough to make AI systems confident mentioning you in comparative context.

Fifth, brands often find that generic content produces generic visibility[44]. Pages with titles like "Features of Our Software" or "Why Choose Us" produce no visibility increase, while pages with specific, data-backed claims—research studies, case studies with precise metrics, original benchmarks—get cited more frequently. This suggests that AI systems prefer specificity and originality over generic positioning. A page saying "we improved customer productivity by 34 percent based on analysis of 1,200 customer accounts" is citable. A page saying "we help businesses be more productive" is not.

Sixth, brands discover that third-party mentions matter more than their own content[44][45]. When you appear in an AI response, the sources cited often aren't your own website—they're G2 reviews, Capterra, LinkedIn profiles, news articles, or industry publications. This reveals a critical insight: your visibility depends partly on what you say about yourself, but heavily on what credible third parties say about you. A brand mentioned once in a major publication might accumulate more visibility than ten mentions on your own blog.

Seventh, many brands discover competitor visibility they didn't expect[4][35]. They might learn through the audit that an obscure competitor they hadn't tracked closely is appearing in AI responses much more frequently. This often leads to investigation revealing that competitor is publishing more authoritative content, getting more media coverage, or has a stronger LinkedIn presence. The audit becomes a competitive intelligence tool revealing which competitors are winning the AI visibility game.

Finally, brands conducting audits often discover that their actual positioning in AI responses differs from their intended positioning[6]. An AI might describe your brand as "affordable option," "startup-focused tool," or "best for small teams" when you position yourself as "enterprise solution." These perception gaps reveal a disconnect between your marketing narrative and the actual content signals the AI is picking up. The misalignment might be due to the customers actually using your tool, the media coverage you've received, or the most prominent mentions existing online.

## Top Three Post-Audit Actions: Turning Findings into Optimization

After completing your audit and documenting findings, you have actionable intelligence to guide your next steps. The most impactful post-audit actions follow logically from what your data revealed.

Your first priority should be addressing visibility gaps in decision-stage queries. If your audit revealed you're invisible when users ask comparative or transactional questions, this is your biggest opportunity. Create specific, comparison-focused content that directly addresses how your solution compares to alternatives and the concrete outcomes you deliver. For example, rather than a page titled "How to Use Our Software," create content titled "Asana vs Monday.com vs ClickUp: Which Is Best for Remote Teams?" with specific comparisons, price tables, and use-case guidance. Structure this content to directly answer the comparative queries where you're currently missing[21][21].

Populate these new pages with data and specificity. Include statistics about your solution's performance, case studies with precise metrics (percentage improvement, specific outcomes achieved, financial impact), and comparisons with named competitors[27][40][44]. The more specific and original your data, the more likely AI systems will cite it in responses[40]. If you can reference research or benchmarks only you possess, that content becomes citable in a way generic content never will be.

Your second priority should be strengthening your earned media presence on the platforms that most influence AI systems. Based on your audit, you discovered which domains get cited when your brand or competitors appear. If your competitors appear in articles on industry publications, TechCrunch, or LinkedIn thought leadership but you don't, you've identified a clear opportunity. Develop a digital PR strategy focused on securing coverage on these trusted publications[45]. When a respected journalist or industry analyst mentions your brand, that mention becomes part of the corpus AI systems draw from when evaluating your credibility.

Specifically, contact journalists covering your industry and offer genuine expertise on emerging trends. Publish original research or surveys on your platform. Contribute guest articles to industry publications. Secure speaking opportunities at conferences and get that coverage written up. Get your executives quoted in news stories. Each of these earned mentions becomes a credibility signal AI systems recognize[45]. Focus on publications that your audit revealed are already being cited for your category—don't waste energy on publications that have no influence over AI responses.

Your third priority should be building topical authority around your core offering. If your audit revealed you lack visibility across related queries, you likely have topical gaps. Create a content cluster around your main topic with pillar pages, supporting cluster pages, and clear internal linking structure[43]. For a project management software company, your pillar page might be "Project Management Software: Complete Guide" with cluster pages addressing "Best Project Management Tools for Remote Teams," "Project Management for Construction," "Agile Project Management Tools," and so on. Each cluster page targets a specific query variation and links back to the pillar[43].

This content structure serves two purposes. First, it helps Google recognize you as a topical authority, improving your organic search visibility[43]. Second, it gives AI systems more pages indexed about your topic, increasing the likelihood they have something from you to cite when answering queries[43]. When AI systems crawl your site, they find comprehensive coverage of your topic rather than scattered pages. This comprehensive coverage signals expertise that single pages cannot.

Beyond these three priorities, implement the learnings from your competitive benchmarking. If you identified a competitor that's outperforming you, study what they're doing. Where are they getting media coverage? What content are they publishing? How are they described in AI responses? Use these observations to inform your strategy. You're not copying their approach—you're learning from their success and adapting it to your own brand and audience.

Finally, schedule a repeat audit in ninety days after you've implemented these actions. The initial audit created a baseline. The follow-up audit reveals whether your changes are moving the needle on AI visibility. Tracking changes over time is more valuable than any individual measurement because it shows whether your optimization efforts are working. If your mention frequency increases from forty-five percent to fifty-five percent, that's directional evidence your changes are helping.

## Conclusion: Building Sustainable AI Visibility Measurement into Your Workflow

Conducting your first LLM brand visibility audit requires effort—realistically, expect to invest eight to twelve hours across the project if you're thorough[4][4][16]. But this investment produces a foundation for understanding your visibility in one of the fastest-growing discovery channels for consumers. Unlike paid tools that require ongoing subscriptions, this DIY approach costs only your time and produces repeatable, defensible results.

The value of this audit extends beyond the initial metrics. You've built a repeatable process you can implement quarterly to track trends. You've created a structured way to think about your brand's position across AI systems. You've identified specific gaps where your competition is outperforming you. You've gathered evidence to justify investment in AI visibility optimization with your leadership team.

Most importantly, you've acknowledged a strategic reality: AI search visibility is not a future concern—it's a current reality shaping how consumers discover, evaluate, and decide on products and services. Brands that wait for perfect tools or complete industry standardization will find themselves behind competitors who started measuring and optimizing earlier. The brands winning the AI search visibility game today are those who began understanding and tracking this channel when it was less obvious, building early-mover advantages that are difficult to overcome.

Your next step is simple: pick a date within the next two weeks, allocate a few hours to designing your query set, and begin testing. Document everything systematically. Calculate your core metrics. Share your findings with your team. Then identify which of the three post-audit priorities applies most directly to your situation and start working on it. The compound effect of consistent measurement and focused optimization will position your brand for visibility in an AI-mediated search landscape where attention and credibility flow to brands that understand how to earn trust from both humans and algorithms.
